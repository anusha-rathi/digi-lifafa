# research/life-events.md

Five ceremonies: griha pravesh, godh bharai, mundan, namkaran, annaprashan.
Agent research 1 September 2026. Full return in the session transcript; this
is what the posts are built on.

## The structural finding, with a number

Seven griha-pravesh gift listicles diffed on 7-gram shingle overlap. Pairwise
Jaccard **0.0000 to 0.0012**, and the only shared strings were WordPress
comment-form boilerplate. **They are independently written.** A plagiarism
checker finds nothing.

But they converge on one claim-set:

| Claim | Pages carrying it, of 7 |
|---|---|
| Ganesh or Lakshmi idol | 6 |
| Silver | 6 |
| Diya | 5 |
| Money plant, tulsi, bamboo | 4 |
| **Cash mentioned at all** | **2** |

Not copying. Every page is written backwards from an inventory, and **cash is
on 2 of 7 because nobody sells cash.** Structural.

**Mirror taboo is imported.** Across 14 pages, "mirror" appears once, on an
India TV listicle citing no Vastu text and no named expert. Knives 3/7, clocks
3/7. Knife has some Indian footing; clock and mirror read as imports from
Chinese gift-taboo lists. Not printed as Indian.

## Griha pravesh

Best source is a forum post, not a page: `kgopalan`, a Tamil Brahmin ritual
specialist answering on tamilbrahmins.com since 2011, posted the full
procedure and the whole gift economy. Thread 60245, June 2026.

**Muhurat, and there are two different systems.**
- Tamil (kgopalan): avoid **Aani, Purattasi, Aadi, Margazhi, Panguni**. Good
  days Mon/Thu/Fri. Avoid lagnas mesham, kadagam, thulam, makaram. And
  **"there should not be guru and sukran asthamanam"**, Jupiter or Venus
  combust.
- North: avoid **Chaturmas**, when Vishnu sleeps. And two panchang sites give
  it **nine days apart** for 2026: gopuja 16 Jul to 17 Nov, panchangbodh
  25 Jul to 20 Nov.
- Tuesday and Saturday avoided in the north; Monday, Thursday, Friday
  preferred in the Tamil list. Compatible, not the same rule.
- kgopalan contradicts himself twice in one post, and then cites the Kanchi
  Mutt panchangam to permit a Sunday his own rule excluded.
- Date chosen against the **birth star of the woman who will live there**.

**The apartment exemption, in his own words:** *"For individual house in
ground floor cow pooja can be done at the entrance. For flats the cow and calf
i think cannot go to upstairs. Better skip cow pooja for flat owners."*
A ritual specialist writing the exemption himself, hedged with "i think".

**Milk boiling over is attested everywhere and under every name**: Paal Kachal
(Kerala), Kitchen Puja inside Bastu Puja (Bengali), inside Vastu Shanti
(Marathi). Safe to state flat.

**The exchange, from kgopalan.** Not a gift list, a two-way ranked exchange:
temple donation at the kula deivam a week before; in-laws arrive with brass
kudam, kuthu vilakku, saree and dhoti, sweets, murukku; **you return saree and
dhoti with thamboolam, fruit, flowers, "with some money"**; siblings bring and
receive the same; dakshinai plus a 9x5 dhoti to each of two to four sastrigals;
a thamboolam bag to every guest; **dhotis or shirt lengths to the contractor,
mason, painter, electrician, carpenter**, plus a meal. He repeats the
means-scaling caveat three times, once as *"This is a public forum. You can
skip or add according to the financial position."*

**Sikh Rehat Maryada, Article XX, primary source, exact:** *"on every happy or
sad occasion, such as moving into a new house, setting up a new business
(shop), putting a child to school, etc., a Sikh should pray for God's help by
performing the Ardas."* And Article XVIII(g): consulting horoscopes for an
auspicious day *"is a sacrilege. Any day that the parties find suitable by
mutual consultation should be fixed."* Contested in practice, and the post
says so: the code is prescriptive, plenty of Punjabi Sikh families do consult
dates.

## Godh bharai

**Name to region.** Godh bharai north; **Seemantham** the religious samskara
in Tamil and Telugu; **Valaikappu** the Tamil bangle function; **Dohale
Jevan** Marathi; **Shaadh** Bengali; **Shrimant / Khodo Bharvo** Gujarati;
**Agharni** Parsi.

**Correction to my brief:** Shrimant is not exclusively Marathi. Gujarati
Hindus and Parsis both use it. The Marathi-specific word is Dohale Jevan.

**Bengal can have three**: 5th month Panchammrita, 7th Saptammrita, main one
in the 9th. Everyone else has one.

**Seemantham vs Valaikappu**: religious samskara, women only, priest, vs the
cultural bangle celebration that men attend. Venue contested: girl's family
home vs the paternal grandmother's, in the same state.

**The sharpest exclusion is not about gender.** Gujarati Shrimant: the woman
doing the coconut exchange must be *"an older sister-in-law or a family member
that has not had a miscarriage."*

**The don't-buy-before-birth taboo is two beliefs, not one.**
- Version A, attributed, USC Folklore, informant 70, Rajasthan: new clothes
  are avoided for **the first few weeks after birth**, and hand-me-downs used,
  *"passing on the luck of the previous surviving baby"*. This is about after
  birth, not before.
- Version B, the don't-buy-anything belief: attested but unattributable to any
  region. Explained by historical infant mortality.
- **The escape hatch is structural**: ceremony gifts are framed as for the
  mother, not the child. Bangles, sarees, food. The taboo bites on things for
  the baby.
- Workarounds people use: keep purchases at your mother's house, leave
  packages sealed, or let the godh bharai carry it.

**Cash is barely attested for this ceremony at all.** Clearest instance is the
Parsi *sagan nu envelope*, which goes to **both** parents.

## Mundan

**The age spread is a caste ladder, not disagreement.** Later authorities:
**1st or 3rd year Brahmin, 5th Kshatriya, 7th Vaishya.** The listicles have
flattened it into a range.

Names: Chudakarana, Choulam, **Jawal** (Marathi), **Zarra-kaa-saai**
(Kashmiri Pandit). "Jhadula" could not be attested at all. Not printed.

**Tirumala**: separate kalyana-katta for men, women and infants. Scriptural
basis is Neela Devi covering Srinivasa's wound with her own hair.
**157 tonnes collected in 2019, sold for about $1.6 million.** Israeli
rabbinical authorities banned it for Jewish wigs in 2004 and 2017.

**"Is mundan necessary for a baby girl?"** The answer is in a comment thread,
not an article. indiaparenting.com, Head Shave board, four named women:
- Sneha: *"in some households (like mine) they celebrate a mundan of a boy
  with huge pomp and show! and for a girl they do it at a near by parlour."*
- Prerna: *"They just took a scissor and shaved her themselves at home. No
  puja no proper ceremony nothing!"*
- Tammana: *"Im shocked to listen to wat u ladies are saying! i have never
  heard of such a thing."*
- Radha: do it with the same pomp, others will follow.
Nominally for both. The money and the ceremony are not. And one woman in the
same thread has never seen it. Both printed.

**Kashmiri Pandit, one blog, one author, treated as one household's memory**:
scissors not razor for the first cut, homa essential, hair kept in the house
with walnuts and immersed later at Gangabal. The **paternal aunt received**
gifts of rice and salt rather than giving.

**Guest gifts: 100% shops.** Every result a jewellery or hamper storefront.
Printed as the finding.

## Namkaran

**When**: 6th night Chhathi (Bihar, Jharkhand, UP, Gujarat, Parsi); 11th or
12th most Hindu families; **12th Barsa** in Maharashtra, from *barah*;
**Telugu Barasala runs 7 days to 29 months**, which alone kills a single
answer. Sikh: *"as soon as the mother is able to move about and bathe,
irrespective of the number of days."*

**Chhathi is undercovered and unmerchandisable.** Sixth night, Shashti
Maiyya / Vidhata enters around midnight to write the child's destiny, so a
**lamp, a red pen and paper** are left on a wooden plank for her.

**Four communities, four different people with the authority to name**:
Marathi the father's sister, Telugu the father (three times in the ear),
Gujarati the foi, Sikh the Granthi from the Hukam.

**Nakshatra syllables**: 27 nakshatras x 4 padas = 108 syllables. Full table
from Drik Panchang. **It gives a sound, not a spelling.** And there is often a
second name: the rashi name used only in sankalpams, the everyday name
different.

**Sikh alternative**: the Guru Granth Sahib opened at random, the first letter
of that Shabad gives the name. Same problem solved by scripture-lottery
instead of astronomy. The Maryada also rejects the sutak birth-pollution
period, *"prescribed periods of ten, twenty one and forty days"*, which is why
the Sikh rule has no day count.

## Annaprashan and the first birthday

**Gendered month rule, independently attested**: girls odd months 5, 7, 9, 11;
boys even 6, 8, 10, 12.

**Names**: Mukhe Bhaat (Bengal), Choroonu (Kerala, very commonly at Guruvayur,
which has a dedicated Choroonu Hall running daily).

**The maternal uncle is load-bearing across three ceremonies in three language
regions**: Bengali annaprashan he feeds the child on his lap at the maternal
home; Telugu barasala he puts a gold ring dipped in milk and honey on the
tongue; Tamil karnavedha the baby sits on his lap.

**Textual dispute worth printing**: Sankhyayana Grhyasutra recommends fish,
goat or partridge gravy in the first solid food. Manava Grhyasutra is silent
on meat.

**Tray divination** attested for Bengali Mukhe Bhaat specifically. Could not
be established for other communities, and it has close cousins in Korean
doljanchi and Chinese zhuazhou. Not generalised to India.

**First birthday**: the traditional form is **Ayush Homam**, reckoned by
nakshatra not the Gregorian date, marking survival of a dangerous first year.
Tamil Brahmin forum: *"All expenses must be borne by the paternal grandfather"*
while the maternal grandfather brings the gifts. An explicit allocation of who
pays and who gives, found nowhere else.

**A firsthand account with dissent**: "deponti", Iyer in Bangalore, moved her
daughter's Ayushya Homam to a Sunday for convenience, and objected on the spot
when a friend performed drishti parihara, on the grounds that it implied her
own guests were envious of her child.

**Candles are lit, not blown out**: *"blowing off the light means cutting
short life"*. Folk rationalisation, attributed as such.
